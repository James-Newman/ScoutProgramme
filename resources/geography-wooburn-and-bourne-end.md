# Geography: Wooburn Green & Bourne End

Used in: [Community Research](../year-1/term-1/community-research.md) — one of five local briefing documents patrols choose from for their World Challenge Award research. Covers location, landscape and connections of Wooburn Green and Bourne End.

## Where are we?

Wooburn Green and Bourne End are villages in south Buckinghamshire, looked after by Buckinghamshire Council. Bourne End is about five miles south-east of High Wycombe and three miles east of Marlow, right on the edge of Berkshire, exactly where the River Wye flows into the River Thames. Wooburn Green sits a little further inland, next to Beaconsfield, Loudwater, Flackwell Heath and Wooburn Common.

Together with smaller hamlets like Berghers Hill, Cores End, Hawks Hill, Widmoor and Wooburn Moor, these villages form one parish called Wooburn and Bourne End. Just over 10,000 people lived here at the last census.

## What does the land look like?

The area sits at the southern edge of the Chiltern Hills — a range of chalk hills that stretches across four counties: Buckinghamshire, Oxfordshire, Hertfordshire and Bedfordshire. The Chilterns have a steep side (called a scarp) and a much gentler side (called a dip slope) that slopes down towards the Thames. Bourne End and Wooburn Green sit on this gentler side.

> Did you know? The land near the Thames is only about 28 metres above sea level, but it climbs to around 112 metres near Juniper Hill — that's almost as tall as the London Eye!

Underneath the ground is chalk, covered with a mix of loam, gravel and sand. This is exactly the kind of rock and soil you'd expect to find across the wider Chilterns area.

## Rivers

The River Wye (sometimes also called the Wickham River) flows through Wooburn. It starts near West Wycombe and travels about ten miles through High Wycombe, Loudwater and Wooburn before joining the much bigger River Thames at Bourne End. Because it's a chalk stream, its water comes mostly from underground rather than rainwater running straight off fields — so it tends to stay clear and flow fairly steadily.

The Thames forms the southern edge of the area. It has been important for transport and milling for centuries, and today Bourne End is a popular spot for sailing and rowing.

## How the land is used

In the past, the local economy relied on watermills along the Wye, farming, and two wharfs (loading points) on the Thames. Today, the area is a mix of houses, some farmland and woodland, and protected green spaces. Big chunks of the surrounding countryside are protected as Green Belt, Chilterns Area of Outstanding Natural Beauty, or a Site of Special Scientific Interest — special labels that limit how much new building can happen there.

## Getting around

The area has decent transport links for its size. Bourne End railway station connects to the main line at Maidenhead, giving access to London Paddington. It first opened in 1854 as part of Isambard Kingdom Brunel's Wycombe Railway, originally under the name Marlow Road.

Wooburn Green used to have its own station too, but it closed in 1970. Even so, the village is close to the M40 motorway, making it easy to reach both London and Birmingham by road.

## Why so many odd little place names?

Unlike villages that grew from one single centre, this parish grew out of lots of small separate hamlets — Cores End, Eghams Green, Upper Bourne End and Well End among them. They slowly merged together as the railway, and later new roads, encouraged building around the station. That's why you'll still spot so many different named areas packed into one small parish today.
