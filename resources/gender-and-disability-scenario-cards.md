# Gender & Disability Scenario Cards

Used in: [Beliefs, Attitudes & Values](../year-1/term-1/beliefs-attitudes-and-values.md) ("Different societies, different attitudes", printed-examples activity). 40 cards, one set per patrol — short real and realistic examples of how attitudes toward gender and disability have played out across history and around the world, alongside several showing how attitudes and inclusion have since improved. Patrols read through the set, agree their five best examples, then one patrol member reads one aloud to the rest of the room.

## Cards

**Card 1 — The Marathon Runner**
Imagine spending months training for a race. You get up early, run in the rain, and work harder than ever before. Then, on race day, someone tells you that you can't take part, not because of your ability, but because you're a girl. In 1967, that happened to women who wanted to run the Boston Marathon. One runner, Kathrine Switzer, entered anyway. During the race, an official even tried to pull her off the course.

**Card 2 — The Wrong Hand**
Imagine your teacher announcing that from tomorrow you must write with your weaker hand. Every lesson. Every test. Every piece of homework. For many left-handed children in the past, this really happened. Adults believed being left-handed was wrong and forced children to change. Some struggled for years with handwriting and confidence because of a rule they never chose.

**Card 3 — Locked Out**
You arrive at a new sports centre with your friends. Everyone runs inside to start the activity. But you use a wheelchair and there are only stairs. No ramp. No lift. No alternative entrance. Your friends can join in immediately, but you are stuck outside. Sometimes the biggest barrier is not a person's disability. It's the way a place has been designed.

**Card 4 — Banned From Football**
Imagine waking up tomorrow and learning that your favourite sport has been banned for people like you. Not because you broke a rule. Not because it was unsafe. Just because some adults decided that people like you shouldn't play. In England, women's football was banned from FA grounds in 1921, even though huge crowds were coming to watch the matches.

**Card 5 — The Invisible Problem**
A pupil starts at a new school. They rarely answer questions and sometimes seem confused when people speak. Other students assume they are rude or not paying attention. What they don't know is that the pupil has significant hearing loss and misses parts of conversations. Not every challenge is immediately visible.

**Card 6 — Pink and Blue**
Imagine if everyone suddenly decided that green was only for girls and yellow was only for boys. It would seem completely random. Yet many colour rules are exactly like that. Around 100 years ago, pink was often considered a colour for boys and blue for girls. The colours never changed. People's opinions did.

**Card 7 — Football Without Sight**
Most people think you need good eyesight to play football. In blind football, players use a ball that contains rattles so they can hear where it is. They communicate constantly with teammates and move at surprising speeds. People watching for the first time are often shocked by the skill involved.

**Card 8 — The Lift That Doesn't Exist**
You arrive at school for your first day. Excited, nervous, ready to make friends. Then you discover your classroom is upstairs and there is no lift. Every day, somebody has to help you, or you miss lessons. For some disabled students, something as simple as a missing lift can affect every school day.

**Card 9 — The Famous Doctor**
Imagine loving science and wanting to become a doctor, but every medical school tells you "people like you aren't allowed". That happened to Elizabeth Garrett Anderson because she was a woman. She kept studying, found another route, and eventually became Britain's first female doctor.

**Card 10 — A Different Language**
Imagine moving to another country and suddenly not understanding the language around you. A deaf person travelling abroad can face something similar. Sign language is not universal. British Sign Language and American Sign Language are very different, even though both countries mainly speak English.

**Card 11 — The Team Nobody Expected**
Wheelchair rugby is one of the most physical sports in the world. Players crash into each other, spin across the court and compete fiercely. Many people expect disability sports to be slow or gentle. Then they watch a wheelchair rugby match and completely change their minds.

**Card 12 — The School Uniform Argument**
Suppose your school announced that one group of students could wear comfortable trousers while another group had to wear something less practical. Around the world, school uniform rules have often been different for boys and girls. Some people believe that is fair. Others think everyone should have the same choices.

**Card 13 — The First Woman in Space**
Before 1963, nobody had seen a woman travel into space. Some people thought the job was simply too difficult for women. Then Valentina Tereshkova became the first woman astronaut. She proved that the limitation had been in people's minds, not in women's abilities.

**Card 14 — The Guide Dog**
You're crossing a busy road. Cars are moving, people are rushing past, and you're depending on your guide dog to help keep you safe. Guide dogs are highly trained and give many blind people much greater independence. Yet some people still underestimate how important they are.

**Card 15 — The Toy Shop**
You walk into a toy shop wanting a science kit. A sign points you away because it's in the "boys section". Or perhaps you want an arts set and people tell you that's "for girls". Many toy shops used to divide toys this way. Increasingly, shops are moving away from those labels.

**Card 16 — Not Allowed to Vote**
Imagine being told that no matter how sensible, informed or responsible you are, your opinion doesn't count. For much of history, most women were not allowed to vote in elections. Today, voting is considered a basic democratic right by many people.

**Card 17 — Reading With Your Fingers**
A teenager loses their sight but still dreams of reading books independently. Louis Braille created a system of raised dots that allows blind people to read through touch. He invented it when he was only 15 years old.

**Card 18 — Faster Than Expected**
Many people assume elite wheelchair athletes would be slower than runners. Then they watch a wheelchair race and see athletes travelling at remarkable speeds. Sometimes assumptions disappear as soon as people see what someone can actually do.

**Card 19 — The New Student**
A student with autism joins your class. They might communicate differently, have different interests, or find some situations more difficult. Sometimes people focus on what makes someone different. Other times they discover they have far more in common than they expected.

**Card 20 — The Wrong Clothes**
A Scottish man wearing a kilt may not think twice about it. Yet visitors from other countries sometimes find it unusual because they associate skirts with women. It raises an interesting question: how many rules about clothing are actually culture rather than fact?

**Card 21 — The Loud Classroom**
Imagine trying to learn in a classroom where you can only hear half of what people are saying. Every missed word makes it harder to follow the lesson. For students with hearing impairments, small changes such as microphones or captions can make a huge difference.

**Card 22 — An Athlete's Dream**
The first Paralympic-style competition in 1948 involved just 16 athletes. Today, Paralympians compete in front of global audiences. It shows how people's attitudes can change when they begin to see talent and achievement instead of limitations.

**Card 23 — The New Bus Route**
Your friends can all travel around town independently. You can't because the buses aren't accessible. Suddenly you need help for journeys that others don't think twice about. Public transport design can make the difference between independence and exclusion.

**Card 24 — The Firefighter**
Imagine wanting to be a firefighter but being told you couldn't because of your gender. For many years, some fire services refused to recruit women. Today, women serve as firefighters around the world.

**Card 25 — The Missed Joke**
Everyone in your group laughs. You smile too, but you have no idea what was said because you didn't hear it clearly. Missing out on little moments can make someone feel isolated, even when they're surrounded by people.

**Card 26 — Beauty Through Time**
Imagine showing a modern hairstyle to teenagers from 300 years ago. They might think it looked ridiculous. Just as we might think the same about theirs. Ideas about appearance constantly change across time and cultures.

**Card 27 — Not One Entrance**
Imagine your school had two entrances. One was easy, quick and direct. The other involved a long detour around the building. Many disabled people experience this sort of difference every day if buildings are not designed with accessibility in mind.

**Card 28 — The Classroom Debate**
A pupil who struggles with reading discovers audiobooks. Suddenly stories become exciting instead of frustrating. Accessibility tools are often designed for one group but end up helping many others too.

**Card 29 — The University Gates**
For centuries, some universities only admitted men. No matter how clever a woman was, she could be turned away. When those rules changed, society gained talented scientists, doctors, teachers and leaders who had previously been excluded.

**Card 30 — Hidden Challenges**
Two students might achieve exactly the same result on a test. One found it easy. The other had to work around dyslexia, anxiety or another challenge that nobody else could see. We often don't know what battles other people are facing.

**Card 31 — The Match Everyone Wanted**
During the First World War, women's football matches attracted huge crowds. Yet despite their popularity, many people still believed girls shouldn't play football. Public opinion and official rules didn't always match.

**Card 32 — The Different Greeting**
You reach out to shake someone's hand, but they bow instead. Neither person is wrong. Around the world, greetings vary enormously. Something that feels completely normal in one country can seem unusual somewhere else.

**Card 33 — The Seat Nobody Thought About**
Many classroom desks were designed for right-handed students. Left-handed pupils had to twist awkwardly or work less comfortably every day. Sometimes people are excluded not because anyone meant to exclude them, but because nobody thought about their needs.

**Card 34 — The Pilot**
Imagine boarding a plane and hearing someone complain because the pilot is a woman. Early female pilots faced exactly that sort of reaction. Over time, people realised flying safely depends on skill and training, not gender.

**Card 35 — The Team Game**
In blind football, players must trust each other completely. They rely on teamwork and communication in ways that sighted players often don't. Many spectators leave with a new respect for the sport.

**Card 36 — The Wrong Assumption**
A person who uses a wheelchair arrives at an event. Someone immediately starts talking to their friend instead of them. Disabled people often report being underestimated because others make assumptions before getting to know them.

**Card 37 — The Colour of Mourning**
In Britain, black is often associated with funerals. In some other cultures, white may be used instead. Customs that seem obvious to us are often just one way among many ways of doing things.

**Card 38 — The School Trip**
The whole year group is excited about a school trip. Then one student discovers parts of the venue are inaccessible. Suddenly something everyone else sees as exciting becomes stressful. Inclusion is often about planning ahead so nobody is left out.

**Card 39 — The Mount Everest Climber**
Many people believed reaching the top of Mount Everest would be impossible for a blind person. In 2001, Erik Weihenmayer proved otherwise. His climb challenged assumptions around the world about what blind people could achieve.

**Card 40 — The First Impression**
When Paralympic athletes compete, many spectators arrive thinking about disability. By the end, they are usually talking about speed, strength, skill or determination. Sometimes seeing what people can do is the quickest way to change minds.

## Notes

- Facts are simplified for a Scout audience — patrols keen to dig deeper should treat these as a starting point, not the final word, matching the approach used for [Global Issues Briefing Cards](global-issues-briefing-cards.md).
- The cards deliberately mix genuine historical facts (named individuals, dates) with short "imagine if" hypotheticals — encourage patrols to pick a spread of both types rather than five of the same kind, for a stronger read-out.
- Print one full set of 40 per patrol rather than splitting the deck between patrols — the activity depends on each patrol seeing the whole set before narrowing down to their five.
