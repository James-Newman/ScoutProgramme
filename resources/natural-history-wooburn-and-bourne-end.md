# Natural History: Wooburn Green & Bourne End

Used in: [Community Research](../year-1/term-1/community-research.md) — one of five local briefing documents patrols choose from for their World Challenge Award research. Covers wildlife and habitats of the Wye Valley and River Thames corridor.

## What is a chalk stream?

The River Wye, which flows through Wooburn and into the Thames at Bourne End, is a special kind of river called a chalk stream. Instead of being fed mainly by rain running straight off the land, chalk streams get most of their water from rain that has soaked deep into the ground and travelled slowly through chalk rock underneath.

This makes the water unusually clear and cool, all year round. That is great news for wildlife that cannot survive in muddy or warm water.

> Did you know? Chalk streams are seriously rare! Most of the world's chalk streams are found in England, and a good number of them are right here in the Chilterns.

## Fish in the Wye

Brown trout live and breed in the Wye. The calmer parts of the river act like a nursery, where baby fish (called fry) can grow safely before swimming out into the much bigger River Thames. For years, old weirs and blockages in the river stopped fish from swimming where they needed to go. Conservation groups have been removing these blockages and adding gravel, so fish can move more freely and lay their eggs in better spots.

## Birds to spot

- Kingfishers — bright blue and orange, fast fliers, hard to spot but unmistakable
- Herons — tall, grey, standing very still at the water's edge
- Mallards, swans and egrets — often seen on the water itself
- Wood pigeons and jays — heard and seen in the woodland areas nearby

Mammals in the area include badgers, voles, muntjac deer and several kinds of bat, which come out at dusk to catch insects flying over the water.

## Warren Nature Reserve

Warren Nature Reserve in Wooburn is a small nature reserve, about the size of three football pitches. It is looked after by the local council together with Wooburn and Bourne End Parish Council. The River Wye runs along one edge, giving ducks, herons and kingfishers somewhere to live, while the reserve's trees — ash, lime and oak — are home to badgers, voles, grass snakes, muntjac deer and bats.

> Did you know? Warren Nature Reserve is a great spot for a Scout wildlife survey — it's small enough to explore properly in one visit but packed with different habitats: river, woodland and open ground.

## Bourne Valley: reptile central

A little further afield, the Bourne Valley is protected as a Site of Special Scientific Interest, which means it is officially recognised as an important place for nature. It is famous for reptiles — in fact, all six of Britain's native reptile species have been recorded there: the common lizard, sand lizard, slow worm, grass snake, adder and smooth snake. That is an impressive line-up for one small area!

## How the area changes through the year

- Spring — birds build nests along the riverbank, and mayflies hatch (a favourite food for trout)
- Summer — plants grow thick along the banks, giving young fish places to hide
- Autumn — leaves fall into the water and trout begin spawning (laying their eggs)
- Winter — rainwater tops up the chalk underground, and the river can rise, sometimes flooding low ground near the Thames

## Try this yourself

- Take a slow walk along the Wye or through Warren Nature Reserve and make a tally chart of every bird you see.
- Look under logs or in leaf litter (carefully, and put everything back) for signs of insects or small creatures.
- Visit the same spot in two different seasons and compare what you notice.
