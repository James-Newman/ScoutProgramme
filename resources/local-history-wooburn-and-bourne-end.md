# Local History: Wooburn Green & Bourne End

Used in: [Community Research](../year-1/term-1/community-research.md) — one of five local briefing documents patrols choose from for their World Challenge Award research. Covers Wooburn, Wooburn Green & Bourne End, Buckinghamshire.

## How did the names come about?

Wooburn and Bourne End are both named after water. A "bourn" is an old English word for a small stream or river. Wooburn sits next to the River Wye, and Bourne End marks the exact spot where the Wye flows into the much bigger River Thames — so its name literally means "the end of the stream".

The name has not always been spelled the way it is today. On a map from 1722 it was written as "Born End". Later, people in the area started pronouncing it strangely, and it even got written down as "Bone End" by mistake! In 1858, the local vicar managed to get the name changed back to the correct "Bourne End", which is still used today.

> Did you know? An old map from 1722 shows the village spelled "Born End" — nothing like how we spell it now!

Before it became the village we know today, Bourne End was actually a group of tiny hamlets dotted along the valley. They had names like Cores End, Spring Gardens, Eghams Green and Upper Bourne End. Most people who lived there worked on farms or in the mills beside the river.

## Wooburn House and the Old Manor

Long ago, a large area of land called "the manor of Wooburn" belonged to important and powerful people. It is even said to have once belonged to Earl Harold, who later became King Harold, before the Norman Conquest of 1066! After that, it passed through the hands of bishops, lords and several noble families, before finally being owned by the Du Pré family for many generations.

Wooburn House, the grand house at the centre of the manor, was rebuilt in 1750. A king, William III, is even said to have visited it. A bishop called William Smyth, who founded Brasenose College at Oxford University, died there too.

## The mills that built the village

For hundreds of years, the River Wye was used to power watermills. This was very important for the local area, because the mills gave people jobs and helped the villages grow. At first, the mills ground corn into flour. Later, many of them changed to making paper and millboard (a thick, stiff type of cardboard) instead.

- Soho Mill and Glory Mill were two of the biggest paper mills.
- Other mills included Jackson's Mill (also called Gunpowder Mill), Princes Mill, Hedsor Mill and Lower Mill.
- Glory Mill kept making paper until the year 2000, when it finally closed.
- Soho Mill closed even earlier, in the 1980s.

After the mills shut down, the old buildings and land were used to build new houses and business parks instead.

## The railway changes everything

The biggest change to the area happened because of the railway. In 1854, the famous engineer Isambard Kingdom Brunel built a railway line connecting Maidenhead to High Wycombe, running straight through the valley.

> Did you know? The engineer who built the railway through Bourne End, Isambard Kingdom Brunel, is one of the most famous engineers in British history. He also designed the SS Great Britain, one of the first iron steamships!

The new station was built out in the fields, between all the little separate hamlets. At first it was called Marlow Road. Then, in 1874, it was renamed Bourne End. Slowly, the area around the new station grew into the village centre we recognise today, rather than any of the old hamlets. A different station, called Wooburn Green, served that village until the railway line closed in 1970.

## From old hamlets to modern villages

During the 1900s, Bourne End and Wooburn Green kept growing. As the paper mills closed down, the land was used for new houses and shops instead. In 2005, the official name of the parish was changed from just "Wooburn" to "Wooburn and Bourne End", showing how much both villages had grown in importance.

Even today, history still affects the local area. A field called Slate Meadow, which separates Bourne End from Wooburn Green, was protected from being built on back in the 1990s after local people campaigned to save it. Part of it has since been given permission for new homes — but as you'll find in [Ancient History](ancient-history-wooburn-and-bourne-end.md), digging there uncovered some very surprising ancient discoveries!
