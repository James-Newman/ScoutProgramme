# Ancient History: Wooburn Green & Bourne End

Used in: [Community Research](../year-1/term-1/community-research.md) — one of five local briefing documents patrols choose from for their World Challenge Award research. Covers prehistoric, Roman and Saxon evidence from Wooburn and Bourne End.

## The earliest evidence: Stone Age finds

The parish of Wooburn has produced a surprising amount of prehistoric material, much of it recovered from gravel terraces beside the Thames and Wye. Two Lower to Middle Palaeolithic hand axes — among the oldest types of tool made by early humans — were found while digging a septic tank on New Road, Bourne End, and another was uncovered during the building of a housing estate at Wooburn Green. A Mesolithic pick turned up during drainage works at Camden Lane, and regular dredging of the Thames has produced Mesolithic flint tranchet axe-heads, cores, flakes and picks over the years.

## Bronze and Iron Age discoveries

Later prehistoric finds include Early Bronze Age daggers, Middle Bronze Age rapiers (a type of thrusting sword) and even a Late Bronze Age sword recovered from the Thames during dredging. A flat-bottomed oak dugout boat, thought to date to the Bronze Age or Iron Age, was found in the river near the railway bridge in the nineteenth century, though it has since been lost. Neolithic to Bronze Age flint flakes have also been found on dry land, for example during the digging of a water pipeline on Wash Hill.

More recently, archaeological work ahead of new housing on Slate Meadow — the field between Bourne End and Wooburn Green — uncovered a roughly 4,000-year-old aurochs (an extinct wild ox) bone along with prehistoric flint tools and Mesolithic artefacts, delaying construction while the site was properly investigated.

## The Roman period

Although the area does not have a major Roman settlement, it has produced scattered evidence of Roman activity. Roman pottery and tile fragments were found during the construction of the railway cutting at Bourne End in the nineteenth century, and further Roman pottery and tile sherds turned up along the route of a water pipeline on Wash Hill. Roman coins have been found at Wooburn Green, and Roman pottery has been recovered from Wooburn churchyard. A possible Roman road from London towards south Oxfordshire may have run across the north of the parish, which would help explain this scattering of material.

The most striking Roman find is a small burial site on Blind Lane, Bourne End, discovered during groundworks shortly after the Second World War. Three burials were found there, two of them in lead coffins — an expensive form of burial usually reserved for wealthier individuals. One coffin, when excavated, was found to contain bird bones placed near the head of the body.

## The Anglo-Saxon cemetery

Evidence for early Anglo-Saxon activity at Bourne End comes from a cemetery dated to the seventh or eighth century AD, first noted before the Second World War. Its existence was initially uncertain, but later research using old notes and illustrations confirmed a genuine cemetery of this period, described in an academic article as reinstated evidence. Related finds from nearby Jackson's Mill include an early Saxon sword blade with a curved back, a type of weapon known as a seax, which was often buried with high-status individuals during the seventh century.

## How archaeologists found out

Very few of these discoveries came from planned archaeological digs. Most were found by accident: a workman digging a septic tank, engineers cutting a railway line, labourers laying a water pipeline, or dredgers clearing the Thames all stumbled across objects that were centuries or millennia old. This is typical of archaeology in ordinary English villages — ancient material is often only found when the ground is disturbed for an entirely different, modern reason, which is exactly what happened during the recent Slate Meadow housing development.

## Piecing the picture together

Taken together, these finds show that people have been living in, working on, and travelling through the Wye valley for many thousands of years — from Stone Age hunters leaving flint tools on the riverbank, through Bronze and Iron Age communities using the Thames as a route for boats and losing weapons in the water, to Roman travellers and Anglo-Saxon settlers who buried their dead on the higher ground above the river. Many of these discoveries were made by chance, during building work, drainage projects or railway construction, which is a good reminder that ancient history can still be hiding just beneath the surface of an ordinary garden or field. For a Scout research project, this is a good example of how ancient history is not just something read about in books — it is physically under the local area, waiting to be noticed.
